# Funny Story

## The Great IKEA Catastrophe of 2023

My girlfriend, Sarah, had been gone for exactly four hours when I decided to build her a bookshelf.

This was not a decision born of kindness or romantic gesture. It was born of guilt. See, three days earlier, I had used her expensive hair dryer to thaw a frozen chicken, and it had exploded in a shower of sparks and feathers. The dryer was dead, the chicken was ruined, and Sarah had looked at me with the quiet disappointment of a woman reconsidering her life choices.

"I don't even know where to start," she had said, holding the smoking remains of what was once a €200 Dyson.

"The chicken was really frozen," I had offered weakly.

"There was a sink, Tom. There was running water. There was a microwave."

"The microwave gives it a weird texture."

She had walked away without another word. That silence was worse than any shouting. It was the silence of a person who was updating their relationship status in their head.

So when Sarah left for her sister's bridal shower, I saw my chance at redemption. I would build her something beautiful. Something useful. Something that said "I am a functional adult who deserves to be in a loving partnership."

I drove to IKEA with the confidence of a man who has never built anything in his life, which is to say, I drove there like I owned the road.

### Part I: The Acquisition

IKEA on a Saturday is a special kind of hell. It is a maze designed by someone who hates humanity and loves watching families slowly disintegrate under fluorescent lighting. I walked in with a mission. I walked past the living room displays. I walked past the kitchen displays. I got lost in the bedroom section for forty-five minutes and considered just buying a mattress and calling it a day.

But no. I was a man on a mission. I found the bookshelf aisle. There were seventeen different models, each with a name that sounded like a Viking war cry. KALLAX. BILLY. HEMNES. I wanted something that said "I have my life together." I chose BJÖRKLIBSÅNG because it looked nice in the display and the name was fun to say.

I grabbed a flat box the size of a small car. It weighed approximately the same as my self-esteem after the hair dryer incident. I dragged it to the checkout. The cashier, a teenager who looked like he had already seen too much, scanned the box.

"Good luck," he said.

"It's just a bookshelf," I said.

"It always is," he replied. I think about that conversation a lot.

I loaded the box into my car. It barely fit. I had to drive with my face pressed against the steering wheel because the box blocked my rearview mirror. I didn't care. I was bringing home victory.

### Part II: The Unboxing

I laid everything out on the living room floor. This took forty minutes. There were approximately seven thousand pieces, none of which looked like they belonged together. There were screws the size of my fingernail and screws the size of my forearm. There was a mysterious wooden dowel that I would later use as a back scratcher. There was an Allen key so small I had to squint to see it.

There was also a piece of wood that did not appear in the instruction manual at all. I set it aside, assuming it was a mistake. (It was not a mistake. I would discover this later, in the middle of the night, when I woke up in a cold sweat realizing what it was for.)

The instructions had no words. This is important. IKEA instructions assume you possess an ancient, primal knowledge of furniture construction that has been passed down through generations. They give you pictograms of stick figures doing things like "insert tab A into slot B," but the stick figure looks like he's performing an exorcism and I honestly couldn't tell the difference.

There was an arrow. Many arrows. The arrows pointed in directions that did not make sense. One arrow appeared to indicate that a screw should be inserted through the fabric of spacetime itself.

My cat, Whiskers, sat on the couch and watched me with undisguised contempt. She was a rescue cat, and I sometimes wonder if her previous owners gave her up because they caught her judging them.

"You could help," I said.

She blinked slowly. It was a blink that said, "I am watching you fail, and I am enjoying it."

### Part III: The Construction

I started strong. Step one: attach leg A to side panel B using screw X. Easy. I found leg A. I found side panel B. I found screw X. I attached them. It took thirty seconds.

I felt powerful. I felt like a real adult. I considered calling my father to tell him I was finally a man. I considered calling Sarah to tell her I was building the Louvre. I considered calling my ex-girlfriend to show her what she was missing. (I did not call anyone. But I thought about it.)

Step two: insert dowels C, D, and E into pre-drilled holes on panel F.

I looked at the dowels. I looked at the holes. There were twelve holes and three dowels. Which holes? The instructions were not clear. The stick figure had no answers. He just stood there, holding a dowel, looking smug. I imagined him as Swedish and morally superior.

I chose three holes at random. This was my first mistake. This was the butterfly flapping its wings that would later become a hurricane of furniture failure.

Step three: attach panel F to side panel A using screws Y and Z.

I did this. It was harder than it looked. The panels did not want to join. They resisted. They had opinions. They had formed a union and were demanding better working conditions.

I applied force. This is the universal solution to all problems when you do not know what you are doing. My father once told me, "If it doesn't fit, hit it harder," and while this advice has ruined many things in my life, it felt appropriate here.

I hit it. It fit. Victory.

By step seven, I realized the shelf was not a rectangle. It was a parallelogram. A confused, drunken parallelogram that leaned to the left like it had given up on life. The top edge was five centimeters higher than the bottom edge. It looked like it was mid-collapse.

"No problem," I said aloud, to no one. Whiskers watched. She knew. She knew everything.

I disassembled my work. This took another hour because I had used the included hammer to really *convince* some of the dowels to go where I wanted them. They did not want to come out. We had a fight. I won, but I lost. You know the feeling.

Step twelve required the Allen key. By this point, my fingers were covered in blisters and I had developed a personal vendetta against a Swedish furniture company that I am convinced is actually run by sadistic engineers who laugh at our suffering. I tightened screws with the fury of a man possessed. The shelf groaned. Whiskers left the room. I was alone.

At some point, I realized I had attached the back panel to the front. I don't know how. I stared at my creation. It stared back. It had no eyes, but I felt judged. The back panel had these little holes in it, and I had screwed them into... somewhere. I don't know. I still don't know.

### Part IV: The Breaking Point

I decided to take a break. I ordered pizza. The delivery guy arrived and looked at the disaster zone that was my living room — screws everywhere, wooden panels scattered like the ruins of an ancient civilization, me sitting cross-legged on the floor, covered in sawdust and shame.

He handed me the pizza and said, "Good luck with that," with the tone of a man who had seen this before. Probably many times. He was probably a therapist in his spare time.

I ate the pizza over the sink. It was the best pizza I had ever eaten. Adrenaline and shame are excellent appetizers. I ate four slices standing up, like an animal, and I regretted nothing.

Reinvigorated, I returned to the battlefield. I consulted YouTube. A cheerful man with an Australian accent built the exact same bookshelf in six minutes. He made it look easy. He probably wrestled crocodiles in his spare time. He probably built his house out of bookshelves. I hated him.

"Nah, mate, just pop it in there, give it a good tap, and you're done," he said, popping and tapping his way to furniture perfection.

I popped. I tapped. Nothing happened.

I watched the video three more times. I slowed it down to half speed. I studied his technique like a film student studying a masterpiece. I noticed he had placed his dowels in completely different holes than I had.

"No," I said. "No, that can't be right."

It was right. He was right. I was wrong. I had been wrong for three hours.

### Part V: The Reckoning

I disassembled everything again. This time I labeled nothing, which was a mistake I will make again. I reassembled from scratch, following the Australian's instructions frame by frame. I paused. I replayed. I wept.

Slowly, impossibly, the bookshelf began to look like a bookshelf. It was not straight. It would never be straight. But it stood. It was vertical. It held the general concept of "shelf."

I added the final shelf board. The moment I let go, the entire structure made a sound. Not a creak. A sound. Like a dying whale.

The shelf sagged in the middle. Not a little sag — a dramatic, theatrical sag, like it was bowing to an audience. The top shelf dipped so low it could have served drinks. It looked like a sad face. The bookshelf was sad. I had built a sad bookshelf.

I tried to fix it. I tightened every screw. I added extra dowels from my "mystery parts" bag — a bag I had convinced myself contained "extras" but actually contained "critical structural components I forgot to install."

Nothing helped. The shelf had chosen its fate. It was going to be a sad, sloping monument to my incompetence.

I stood back and admired my work in the way a man admires a car crash he caused. It was a disaster, but it was *my* disaster. I had created something. It was terrible. But it existed.

### Part VI: The Discovery

Sarah came home three hours early.

I heard her key in the lock and panicked. I stood in front of the shelf, arms spread wide, like I was hiding a dead body. Because in a way, I was. My dignity lay crumpled behind that shelf.

She walked in. She saw me. She saw the shelf. She saw the mess. She saw everything.

"What... is that?" she asked.

"It's a bookshelf," I said. "For your books. Happy... um... Tuesday."

She walked around me. She inspected my work. She pressed a finger to the top shelf, and the entire thing wobbled like a jelly in an earthquake.

"Is it supposed to do that?" she asked.

"It's... expressive," I said. "It has character."

She pressed another shelf. The wobble intensified. The shelf groaned. I swear I heard a screw fall out.

She stared at me. I stared at her. Whiskers came back and stared at both of us. We were a tableaux of mutual disappointment.

Then Sarah started laughing.

Not a polite laugh. Not an "I think I'm going to break up with you" laugh. Not even a "this is kind of funny" laugh. A full, from-the-gut, can't-breathe, tears-streaming-down-her-face, I'm-going-to-pass-out laugh. She laughed so hard she had to sit down. She sat on the floor among the debris of my failure and howled.

"I've been gone six hours," she wheezed. "Six. Hours."

"Yes."

"And you've created... this."

"I call it BJÖRKLIBSÅNG."

She lost it again. She was on the floor, clutching her stomach, gasping for air. Whiskers looked at her like she had lost her mind. Maybe she had.

"It was supposed to be romantic," I said.

She looked up at me, tears in her eyes, nose running, still giggling. "You tried to thaw a chicken with my hair dryer," she said. "And now this."

"I know."

"You're a disaster, Tom."

"I know."

"You're my disaster."

And that's how I learned that sometimes, the best way to win someone's heart is to be spectacularly, historically, hilariously incompetent.

### Epilogue

We kept the shelf.

It lives in the corner of our living room, a permanent reminder that love isn't about being perfect. It's about finding someone who laughs at your disasters instead of leaving you for them.

It holds exactly three books before it threatens to collapse. We call it BJÖRKLIBSÅNG the Unstable. It is our favorite piece of furniture. Every time someone comes over, Sarah tells the story. She tells it better than I do. She includes dramatic reenactments. She is a good storyteller.

The mystery piece of wood that I set aside in the unboxing? I used it six weeks later to patch a hole in the wall that I made while trying to hang a picture. It fit perfectly. Some mysteries are never meant to be solved.

Whiskers still judges me, though. Some wounds never heal.

Sarah bought a proper bookshelf from a real furniture store six months later. She let me assemble it. By then, I'd learned enough to know that instructions exist for a reason, dowels go in specific holes, and the Allen key is not a toy. The new shelf is perfectly straight.

We keep it in the bedroom.

BJÖRKLIBSÅNG the Unstable remains in the living room, a monument to the worst day of my carpentry career and the best day of our relationship.

I still think about that chicken sometimes. I use a microwave now.

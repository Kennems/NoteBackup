# Finance and Investing

## Personal Finance Basics

### Budgeting

Budgeting is the foundation of personal finance. It involves tracking income and expenses to ensure spending aligns with priorities. The most common approaches:

- **50/30/20 Rule**: Allocate 50% of after-tax income to needs (housing, food, utilities), 30% to wants (entertainment, travel), and 20% to savings and debt repayment.
- **Zero-Based Budgeting**: Every dollar is assigned a purpose — income minus expenses equals zero. Requires meticulous tracking but maximizes intentionality.
- **Envelope System**: Cash is divided into envelopes for each spending category. Once an envelope is empty, no more spending in that category until the next cycle.

**Key principles**: Prioritize saving first (pay yourself before bills), review spending monthly, and adjust categories as circumstances change. Budgeting is not about restriction — it is about alignment between spending and values.

### Emergency Fund

An emergency fund is cash set aside for unexpected expenses — job loss, medical emergencies, urgent home or car repairs.

- **Target size**: 3–6 months of essential living expenses for most people. Freelancers or those with variable income should aim for 6–12 months.
- **Where to hold**: High-yield savings account or money market account — liquid, low risk, accessible within 1–2 business days.
- **What qualifies**: Only genuine emergencies. Vacations, electronics upgrades, or non-essential purchases do not count.
- **Rebuilding**: After an emergency withdrawal, prioritize replenishing the fund before resuming other non-essential investing or spending.

The emergency fund is the single most important buffer between you and financial disaster. It should be established before any long-term investing begins.

### Debt Management

Not all debt is equal. Understanding the cost structure of debt is essential.

- **Good debt**: Debt used to acquire appreciating assets (education, real estate) or productive capital. Typically has lower interest rates and tax-advantaged treatment.
- **Bad debt**: High-interest consumer debt — credit cards, payday loans, auto loans for depreciating assets. Interest rates of 15–30%+ destroy wealth.

**Repayment strategies**:

- **Avalanche Method**: Target debts with the highest interest rate first. Mathematically optimal — minimizes total interest paid.
- **Snowball Method**: Target the smallest balance first. Psychologically motivating — quick wins build momentum.

**Key metrics**:

- **Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI)**: Total monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Lenders prefer under 36%.
- **Credit Utilization**: Credit card balances divided by credit limits. Keep under 30% for healthy credit scores.

**Warning signs**: Making only minimum payments, borrowing to pay existing debt, using savings to cover routine expenses.

## Investment Fundamentals

### Risk vs Return

The central idea in investing: **higher potential returns come with higher risk**. There is no free lunch.

- **Risk-Free Rate**: The theoretical return of an asset with zero risk (proxied by short-term government bonds). All other investments must offer a premium above this to justify their risk.
- **Risk Premium**: The extra return investors demand for holding a risky asset over a risk-free one.
- **Volatility** is not the same as risk, but it is the most commonly used proxy. True risk is the permanent loss of capital.

**Risk types**:
| Risk Type | Description | Mitigation |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Market Risk | Broad market declines | Diversification across asset classes |
| Inflation Risk | Purchasing power erosion | Real assets, TIPS, equities |
| Concentration Risk | Overweight in one asset | Rebalancing, diversification |
| Liquidity Risk | Inability to sell quickly | Hold liquid assets, avoid penny stocks |
| Credit Risk | Borrower defaults | Credit ratings, diversification |
| Currency Risk | FX fluctuations | Hedging, global diversification |

**Historical perspective**: The S&P 500 has returned roughly 10% annually on average over the long term, but with significant year-to-year variability. Bonds have returned 5–6%. Cash has barely kept pace with inflation.

### Asset Allocation

Asset allocation is the process of dividing a portfolio among different asset classes. Research suggests it explains over 90% of the variability in portfolio returns — more than individual security selection or market timing.

**Determinants of allocation**:

- **Time Horizon**: Longer horizons allow greater equity exposure to ride out volatility.
- **Risk Tolerance**: Subjective comfort with short-term losses. Must be honestly assessed.
- **Financial Goals**: Retirement, education, house purchase — each has a different timeline and required return.
- **Income Stability**: Stable employment supports higher risk; variable income suggests more conservative positioning.

**Sample allocations**:

| Age / Profile | Stocks | Bonds | Cash / Other |
|---------------|--------|-------|--------------|
| Aggressive (20–30s) | 90% | 10% | 0% |
| Moderate (40–50s) | 70% | 25% | 5% |
| Conservative (60+) | 40% | 50% | 10% |

The classic rule of thumb: **110 minus your age** as the percentage in stocks. This is a starting point, not a prescription.

### Diversification

Diversification reduces unsystematic risk (risk specific to individual companies or sectors) without proportionally reducing expected returns.

**Diversification dimensions**:

- **Across asset classes**: Stocks, bonds, real estate, commodities.
- **Across geographies**: Domestic vs international. US markets are roughly 60% of global market cap.
- **Across sectors**: Technology, healthcare, financials, energy, consumer goods.
- **Across time**: Dollar-cost averaging reduces timing risk.

**The diversification trap**: Over-diversification (holding hundreds of similar assets) provides no additional benefit and increases complexity and costs. 20–30 well-selected stocks or a few broad index funds achieve most diversification benefits.

**Correlation**: The key to effective diversification is holding assets with low or negative correlation. When stocks fall, bonds often rise (though not always — correlations can converge during crises).

## Asset Classes

### Stocks (Equities)

Ownership shares in a company. Stockholders participate in the company's profits through dividends and capital appreciation.

**Characteristics**:
- Highest long-term expected return among major asset classes
- Most volatile in the short term
- Subordinate to bondholders in bankruptcy (higher risk)
- Unlimited upside potential

**Stock classifications**:
- **By size**: Large-cap (>$10B), mid-cap ($2B–$10B), small-cap (<$2B)
- **By style**: Growth (high earnings growth, often expensive), Value (undervalued by metrics, often cheaper), Blend (mix)
- **By sector**: Technology, healthcare, financials, energy, consumer staples, etc.

**Valuation metrics**:
- **P/E (Price to Earnings)**: Price per share / earnings per share. Higher = more expensive.
- **P/B (Price to Book)**: Price per share / book value per share. Useful for financials and value stocks.
- **Dividend Yield**: Annual dividend / price. Income-focused investors prefer higher yields.
- **PEG Ratio**: P/E divided by earnings growth rate. PEG < 1 may indicate undervaluation.

### Bonds (Fixed Income)

Loans to governments or corporations. Bondholders are creditors, not owners.

**Characteristics**:
- Lower expected return than stocks
- Lower volatility than stocks
- Priority over stockholders in bankruptcy
- Fixed interest payments (coupon) with principal returned at maturity

**Bond types**:
| Type | Issuer | Risk Level | Tax Treatment |
|------|--------|------------|---------------|
| Treasury | US Government | Risk-free (nominal) | State tax exempt |
| Municipal | State/Local Gov | Low to moderate | Federal tax exempt |
| Corporate (IG) | Companies | Moderate | Fully taxable |
| Corporate (HY/Junk) | Companies | High | Fully taxable |
| TIPS | US Government | Inflation-protected | State tax exempt |

**Key concepts**:
- **Yield to Maturity (YTM)**: Total return if held to maturity, assuming reinvestment.
- **Duration**: Sensitivity of bond price to interest rate changes. Longer duration = more price volatility.
- **Credit Rating**: AAA (safest) to D (default). Ratings agencies: Moody's, S&P, Fitch.
- **Yield Curve**: Plot of yields across maturities. An inverted yield curve (short-term rates higher than long-term) has historically predicted recessions.

**Interest rate risk**: When rates rise, existing bond prices fall. The longer the maturity, the greater the price decline.

### Real Estate

Tangible property held for income generation or capital appreciation.

**Investment methods**:
- **Direct ownership**: Rental properties, commercial real estate. Requires active management.
- **REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)**: Publicly traded companies that own and operate income-producing real estate. Provide liquidity and dividends.
- **Real Estate Crowdfunding**: Pooled investments in specific properties. Moderate liquidity.

**Advantages**:
- Tangible asset with intrinsic utility
- Potential for leverage (mortgages)
- Tax benefits (depreciation, 1031 exchanges)
- Inflation hedge (rents and property values rise with inflation)

**Disadvantages**:
- Illiquid — cannot sell quickly without significant cost
- High transaction costs (commissions, closing costs, taxes)
- Active management burden (maintenance, tenants, regulations)
- Concentrated risk (property-specific and location-specific)

**Key metrics**:
- **Cap Rate**: Net operating income / property value. Higher = better return (and usually more risk).
- **Cash-on-Cash Return**: Pre-tax cash flow / total cash invested.
- **Gross Rent Multiplier (GRM)**: Property price / gross annual rent. Lower is generally better.

### Commodities

Physical goods — precious metals, energy, agricultural products, industrial metals.

**Characteristics**:
- No cash flows (no dividends, no interest)
- Returns come purely from price appreciation
- Typically negative correlation with stocks and bonds (good diversifier)
- Highly volatile
- Hedges against inflation and geopolitical turmoil

**Major categories**:
| Category | Examples | Drivers |
|----------|----------|---------|
| Precious Metals | Gold, Silver, Platinum | Inflation, currency debasement, fear |
| Energy | Oil, Natural Gas | Supply/demand, geopolitics |
| Agriculture | Wheat, Corn, Soybeans | Weather, crop cycles |
| Industrial Metals | Copper, Aluminum, Iron | Economic growth, industrialization |

**Investment vehicles**: Futures contracts, commodity ETFs, commodity-linked notes, physical ownership (gold bars, coins).

**Gold's special role**: Often called "the barbarous relic," gold has maintained purchasing power over millennia. It performs well during high inflation, currency crises, and geopolitical uncertainty. However, it has no yield and has underperformed equities over long periods.

### Cryptocurrency

Digital assets using blockchain technology. The newest and most controversial asset class.

**Characteristics**:
- Extremely high volatility
- No underlying cash flows (like commodities)
- 24/7 trading, global, permissionless
- Highly speculative — prices driven primarily by sentiment and narrative
- Regulatory uncertainty across jurisdictions

**Major examples**:
- **Bitcoin**: The original cryptocurrency. Fixed supply (21M). Positioned as digital gold.
- **Ethereum**: Smart contract platform. Powers DeFi, NFTs, dApps.
- **Stablecoins**: Pegged to fiat currency (USDT, USDC). Used for trading and payments.
- **Altcoins**: Smaller projects — highly speculative and risky.

**Criticisms**:
- No intrinsic value or cash flow generation
- Extremely energy-intensive (proof-of-work)
- Used for illicit transactions
- Subject to exchange hacks, scams, and rug pulls
- Regulatory bans in several major economies

**Cautious approach**: If investing, limit to a small percentage of the portfolio (1–5%), treat it as pure speculation, and be prepared for total loss.

## Investment Strategies

### Value Investing

Pioneered by Benjamin Graham and popularized by Warren Buffett. Buy securities trading below their intrinsic value.

**Core principles**:
- **Margin of Safety**: Buy at a significant discount to estimated intrinsic value. This discount protects against errors in analysis or adverse events.
- **Mr. Market**: The market is a voting machine in the short term and a weighing machine in the long term. Price fluctuations are opportunities, not signals.
- **Circle of Competence**: Only invest in businesses you deeply understand. Avoid what you cannot analyze.
- **Long-term horizon**: Hold for years or decades. Value investing requires patience.

**Key metrics used**:
- Low P/E and P/B ratios
- High dividend yield
- Strong balance sheet (low debt)
- Consistent earnings
- Owner earnings (Buffett's preferred metric: net income + depreciation + amortization - maintenance capex)

**Famous value investors**: Benjamin Graham, Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger, Seth Klarman, Joel Greenblatt.

**Criticism**: Value investing has underperformed growth investing over the past decade, leading some to declare it "dead." Historically, such periods have preceded strong value rebounds.

### Growth Investing

Invest in companies with above-average revenue or earnings growth potential.

**Core principles**:
- Focus on future potential rather than current valuation
- Accept high P/E ratios (or negative earnings) in exchange for growth
- Look for competitive moats, large addressable markets, and strong management
- Higher risk, but potentially higher reward

**Characteristics of growth stocks**:
- Revenue growing 15–30%+ annually
- Operating in expanding industries (tech, biotech, clean energy)
- Often reinvest all earnings into the business (no dividends)
- Higher volatility and drawdowns during market corrections

**Key metrics**:
- Revenue growth rate
- Total Addressable Market (TAM)
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) vs lifetime value (LTV)
- Gross margin trends
- Market share evolution

**Risk**: Growth stocks are sensitive to interest rate changes (future cash flows are discounted more heavily when rates rise). They also face "show me" risk — high expectations must be met quarter after quarter.

### Index Investing

Passive investing strategy that aims to replicate the performance of a market index rather than beat it.

**Core philosophy** (championed by John Bogle, Vanguard founder):
- Most active managers fail to beat the market over the long term
- Low costs compound significantly over decades
- Tax efficiency matters more than most realize
- Time in the market beats timing the market

**Advantages**:
| Factor | Index Investing | Active Investing |
|--------|----------------|------------------|
| Fees | 0.03–0.10% ER | 0.50–1.00%+ ER |
| Management | None required | Research-intensive |
| Turnover | Low | High |
| Tax Efficiency | High | Low |
| Consistency | Market returns | Inconsistent |

**Popular index funds**:
- **VOO / SPY**: S&P 500 (US large caps)
- **VTI / ITOT**: Total US stock market
- **VT**: Total world stock market
- **BND / AGG**: Total US bond market
- **VXUS**: Total international stock market

**Bogleheads' Three-Fund Portfolio**:
1. Total US Stock Market (VTI) — 60%
2. Total International Stock (VXUS) — 20%
3. Total Bond Market (BND) — 20%

This simple portfolio has consistently outperformed the majority of actively managed funds with a fraction of the effort and cost.

### Other Strategies

- **Dividend Investing**: Focus on companies with consistent and growing dividends. Provides steady income and signals financial health. Examples: Dividend Aristocrats (25+ years of dividend growth).
- **Momentum Investing**: Buy assets with recent strong performance, sell those with weak performance. Works empirically but requires discipline during reversals.
- **Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)**: Invest a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price. Reduces the impact of volatility and eliminates timing risk.
- **Lump Sum Investing**: Invest all available capital immediately. Historically beats DCA ~66% of the time because markets trend upward. Requires strong stomach for potential immediate losses.

## Market Analysis

### Fundamental Analysis

Evaluating a security's intrinsic value by examining economic, financial, and qualitative factors.

**Top-down approach**:
1. Analyze the macroeconomy (GDP, inflation, interest rates, employment)
2. Analyze the industry/sector (competitive landscape, regulation, trends)
3. Analyze the specific company

**Bottom-up approach**: Start with individual companies regardless of macro environment. Focus on business quality, financials, and valuation.

**Key analysis areas**:

| Area | What to Examine |
|------|----------------|
| Financial Statements | Income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement |
| Profitability | Gross margin, operating margin, net margin, ROE, ROIC |
| Growth | Revenue growth, earnings growth, book value growth |
| Efficiency | Asset turnover, inventory turnover, receivable days |
| Liquidity | Current ratio, quick ratio, cash position |
| Solvency | Debt-to-equity, interest coverage ratio |
| Valuation | P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA, dividend yield |

**Qualitative factors**:
- Management quality and track record
- Competitive advantage (economic moat)
- Brand strength and customer loyalty
- Regulatory environment
- Industry tailwinds or headwinds

### Technical Analysis

Analyzing price and volume data to predict future price movements. Based on the premise that all known information is already reflected in the price.

**Core assumptions**:
1. Price discounts everything
2. Prices move in trends
3. History tends to repeat itself (market psychology is cyclical)

**Common tools**:

- **Trend Analysis**: Support and resistance levels, trendlines, channels. "The trend is your friend until it ends."
- **Moving Averages**: Simple (SMA) and Exponential (EMA). Common periods: 50-day, 200-day. Golden cross (50 above 200) = bullish, death cross = bearish.
- **Momentum Indicators**: RSI (Relative Strength Index), MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence). Identify overbought/oversold conditions.
- **Volume Analysis**: Confirms price movements. High volume breakouts are more reliable.
- **Chart Patterns**: Head and shoulders, double top/bottom, flags, triangles, cup and handle.

**Criticism**: Technical analysis is often dismissed as "chart reading" or "astrology for finance." Most academic research finds it does not consistently generate excess returns after accounting for transaction costs and risk.

**Practical use**: Many professional traders combine fundamental and technical analysis — using fundamental analysis for what to buy and technical analysis for when to buy or sell.

## Portfolio Management

### Strategic Asset Allocation

Setting a long-term target allocation and periodically rebalancing back to those targets.

**Steps**:
1. Define investment goals (retirement age, income needs, risk capacity)
2. Choose asset classes and determine long-term expected returns and correlations
3. Set target weights based on risk tolerance and time horizon
4. Implement using low-cost vehicles (index funds, ETFs)
5. Rebalance periodically

**Rebalancing methods**:
- **Calendar rebalancing**: Quarterly or annually. Simple but may miss opportunities.
- **Threshold rebalancing**: Rebalance when an asset class deviates by a fixed percentage (e.g., 5%) from its target. More responsive.
- **Cash flow rebalancing**: Use new contributions or withdrawals to adjust allocation toward targets. Tax-efficient.

**Tax considerations**:
- Hold tax-efficient assets (index funds, stocks with low turnover) in taxable accounts
- Hold tax-inefficient assets (bonds, REITs, actively managed funds) in tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401k)
- Use tax-loss harvesting to offset realized gains

### Active vs Passive Management

**Active management**: Attempt to beat the market through security selection and market timing.
- Higher fees (expense ratios, management fees, performance fees)
- Higher turnover (more transaction costs, tax implications)
- Most active managers underperform their benchmarks over 5- and 10-year periods

**Passive management**: Accept market returns with minimal costs.
- Lower fees — compound savings dramatically over decades
- Lower turnover — tax efficient
- Guaranteed market returns — no manager risk
- Academic backing: Efficient Market Hypothesis (semi-strong form)

**SPIVA Scorecard**: Standard & Poor's Indices vs Active funds consistently shows ~80–90% of active US equity fund managers underperform their benchmark over 10-year periods.

**Middle ground**: Core-satellite approach — hold 80–90% in passive index funds ("core") and 10–20% in carefully selected active investments ("satellite").

### Risk Management

- **Position sizing**: No single position should be large enough to materially harm the portfolio. 5% maximum per individual stock is a common rule.
- **Stop-losses**: Predetermined exit points that automatically sell an asset if it falls below a certain price. More common in active trading than long-term investing.
- **Hedging**: Using options, futures, or inverse ETFs to offset potential losses. Typically reduces returns in exchange for lower volatility.
- **Stress testing**: Model portfolio performance under historical worst-case scenarios (2008 financial crisis, 2020 COVID crash, 2022 inflation shock).
- **Monte Carlo simulation**: Run thousands of random scenarios to estimate probability of meeting goals.

## Behavioral Finance

Behavioral finance studies how psychological biases affect financial decisions. It challenges the traditional assumption of rational economic actors.

### Common Biases

| Bias | Description | Impact on Investing |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Confirmation Bias** | Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs | Ignoring warning signs about a held position |
| **Anchoring** | Fixating on a specific reference point (e.g., purchase price) | Refusing to sell a loser because "it was higher before" |
| **Loss Aversion** | Feeling losses more intensely than equivalent gains (~2x) | Selling winners too early, holding losers too long |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimating one's knowledge or skill | Excessive trading, inadequate diversification |
| **Herd Mentality** | Following the crowd | Buying at market peaks, selling at bottoms |
| **Recency Bias** | Giving more weight to recent events | Assuming recent trends will continue indefinitely |
| **Hindsight Bias** | Believing past events were predictable | "I knew it all along" — leads to overconfident future predictions |
| **Sunk Cost Fallacy** | Continuing a course of action due to past investment | Holding losing positions to avoid "realizing" the loss |
| **Status Quo Bias** | Preference for the current state of affairs | Failure to rebalance or update strategy |
| **Mental Accounting** | Treating money differently based on its source or purpose | Spending a tax refund frivolously while being frugal with salary |

### Practical Implications

**For individual investors**:
- Create a written investment plan and stick to it — reduces impulsive decisions
- Use automation (automatic contributions, automatic rebalancing)
- Limit how often you check portfolio value (quarterly > daily)
- Keep a "diary" of investment decisions with reasoning — review later to identify patterns
- Seek contrariant viewpoints to challenge your thesis

**For portfolio construction**:
- Build portfolios that match your true risk tolerance, not your aspirational risk tolerance
- Acknowledge that you cannot predict the future — diversify accordingly
- Account for your own behavioral weaknesses: if you panic-sell in downturns, hold a more conservative allocation than your age suggests

**The Pareto principle in investing**:
- 80% of outcomes come from 20% of decisions
- The most important decision is the savings rate (how much you save)
- The second most important is asset allocation
- Security selection and market timing are distant third and fourth
